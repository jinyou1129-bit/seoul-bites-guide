Insadong Traditional Tea Houses & Heritage Street Snacks

Insadong rewards a slow wander: step away from the main shopping street into a quiet courtyard or a wooden tea room, then sample warm street snacks and browse craft shops.

Find a traditional tea house

Some tea houses occupy or evoke a hanok, a traditional Korean building, with wooden beams, low tables, paper screens and a courtyard. Interiors vary; not every tea shop is a historic building. Check whether seating is floor-level if accessibility matters, and allow time to sit rather than treating tea as a takeaway stop.

Try omija-cha (오미자차), made from the five-flavor berry: its name refers to sweet, sour, salty, bitter and pungent tastes. It is often served chilled or hot and may be sweetened. Ask how the house prepares it. Jujube tea (daechu-cha) and citron tea (yuja-cha) are other comforting choices; tea menus may change with the season.

Traditional sweets to try

  • Yugwa (유과): a light, crisp traditional sweet often made from glutinous rice and coated in syrup and puffed grains. It can be served beside tea.
  • Yakgwa (약과): a dense, honeyed fried wheat pastry with a gently spiced flavor. Despite being a Korean traditional sweet, it is usually wheat-based rather than a rice sweet.
  • Hotteok (호떡): a griddled filled pancake, often with brown sugar, cinnamon and nuts. A fresh one can be very hot inside: wait briefly before biting. Follow the queue and have a payment method ready.

Ingredients vary. Rice sweets, pastries and fillings can contain wheat, sesame, nuts, honey or other allergens; ask vendors before buying. Not every item is vegetarian.

A relaxed walking plan

  1. Start on Insadong-gil and peek into the lanes branching off the main street. Compare tea-house menus and seating before choosing.
  2. Order a pot or cup of omija-cha and one sweet to share. Ask whether refills or additional hot water are available and whether a minimum order applies.
  3. Continue along the street for hotteok or another snack. Eat carefully and dispose of wrappers in a bin; availability and lines vary by time and day.
  4. Browse galleries, paper shops and craft stalls. If an item is fragile, ask whether it can be wrapped for travel.

Shopping etiquette

Greet the shopkeeper, ask before photographing artwork or touching delicate pieces, and handle ceramics with care. Prices are often displayed; do not assume bargaining is expected in a fixed-price shop. Ask about materials, origin and return policy before paying, particularly for antiques or expensive crafts. Receipts can help if questions arise later.

For neighborhood context and trip planning, consult VISITKOREA; verify individual tea-house hours and current menus directly before visiting.

Frequently asked questions

Is omija tea caffeinated?

Plain omija infusion is fruit-based rather than tea-leaf based, but blends vary. Ask if caffeine matters to you.

Are these sweets gluten-free?

Do not assume so. Yakgwa typically contains wheat, and recipes or cross-contact vary. Ask the seller about ingredients.

Do I need a reservation?

Many casual tea houses accept walk-ins, but popular venues can fill up. Check directly for groups or weekends.

Can I take tea or sweets home?

Packaged items travel more easily than fresh snacks. Check shelf life, packaging and your destination's import rules.
