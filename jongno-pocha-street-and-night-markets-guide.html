Seoul Pocha (Street Food Tents) & Night Markets: The Ultimate Tourist Survival Guide

Kept in this browser only.

Steam on the windows, clinking soju glasses, and strangers laughing over a shared plate: the K-drama scene is real. Here’s how to enjoy it without guessing your way through the menu.

The short version: Start at Jongno 3-ga Station, exits 3 or 4; arrive around 6 PM. Choose a tent, check its menu and prices, share a main dish, and keep cash handy. Hours and payment vary by stall.
Jongno tents · Hipjiro · What to eat · Rules · Show to server

1. Jongno 3-ga Pocha Street: your red-tent moment

A pojangmacha (포장마차), or pocha, is a covered street stall where you can settle in for food and drinks. Around Nakwon Arcade and Donhwamun-ro 11-gil, red and orange vinyl tents sit alongside open-air restaurant tables. Expect steam, close quarters and the hum of conversations — not a single organized market with one menu.

When: Stalls begin setting up around 5–6 PM; roughly 6 PM–2 AM is a useful evening planning window, not a guaranteed schedule. Each vendor sets its own hours and may close earlier or later; weather can affect outdoor seating. For a seat, come early rather than assuming a 2 AM kitchen.

Getting there: Take subway Lines 1, 3 or 5 to Jongno 3-ga Station (종로3가역), Exit 3 or 4. Head toward Donhwamun-ro 11-gil and Nakwon Arcade, following the lit tents. Exit 5 is another useful approach; Korea Tourism Organization also identifies Exit 6 near Nakwon Music Mall. Search 종로3가 포장마차 in Naver or Kakao Map. Check your last train before ordering another round.

2. Euljiro “Hipjiro”: beer under the lights

For a different kind of night, head to Nogari Alley (노가리 골목), where old printing and hardware shops meet the neon of Euljiro’s bars. In good weather, plastic tables spill outside the beer halls (hof); the local classic is cold beer with chewy dried pollack, or nogari. This is an outdoor beer alley, not the same thing as Jongno’s tent stalls.

Getting there: Euljiro 3-ga Station (Lines 2 and 3), Exit 3, then walk toward Nogari Alley. Outdoor tables depend on the venue and weather; check local conditions and each bar’s hours. If it’s full, try a nearby indoor table.

3. What to order: four pocha essentials

Odeng soup · 어묵탕

Fish cakes in a warming broth, sometimes with radish and skewers. A comforting shared starter; contains fish and often wheat.

Spicy stir-fried octopus · 낙지볶음

Chewy octopus and vegetables in a spicy red sauce. Ask about the heat before ordering; it often pairs well with rice.

Grilled chicken skewers · 닭꼬치

Grilled chicken on skewers with a sweet or spicy glaze. More common at snack stalls than every sit-down pocha, so check the menu.

Seafood pancake · 해물파전

A savory scallion pancake with seafood, sliced for sharing. Usually made with wheat and may contain egg or shellfish.

Menus and portions differ by stall. For allergies, ask about ingredients and cross-contact; broth, sauces and shared grills can hide allergens.

4. Pocha rules nobody puts in the drama script

  • Order a main: Many stalls expect at least one shared main dish (anju) per group, often alongside drinks. Requirements vary, so ask before sitting, especially if you only want a drink.
  • Check the price first: Review the displayed menu, portion size and any minimum. Confirm the total before you order and ask before accepting extras.
  • Cash or card? Some take cards, while others may prefer or require cash. Carry Korean won and ask 카드 돼요? (“Can I pay by card?”) before ordering. ATMs may not accept every foreign card.
  • Restrooms: Small tents often have no toilet. Ask the vendor where the nearest public restroom is; a subway station may have one, but access and hours vary. Don’t assume it stays open after the last train.
  • Be considerate: Stay within seating areas, keep walkways clear, and drink responsibly. You can enjoy the food without alcohol.

5. Show to server

Turn your screen toward the server. These cards keep the Korean visible and easy to read.

소주 한 병 더 주세요

Soju han byeong deo juseyo

One more bottle of soju, please.

이모, 계산해 주세요

Imo, gye-san-hae juseyo

Check, please!

Imo is a familiar way to address some servers; 저기요, 계산해 주세요 is a polite alternative if unsure.

Make a night of it

Start with a Jongno tent and a shared pancake, then head to Euljiro for beer and nogari if you have time. For a broader market-food stop, see our Gwangjang Market guide. Confirm market and vendor hours separately; this itinerary does not imply they all stay open late.
